NHS Pension Scheme 2026/27: Contribution Rates & How Much You Pay

If you work for the NHS, one of the most valuable benefits you receive is access to the NHS Pension Scheme — widely regarded as one of the best workplace pensions available in the UK. But with tiered contribution rates, tax relief rules, and the switch from Final Salary to the CARE scheme, it can feel complicated. This guide breaks everything down clearly, covering NHS pension contribution rates 2026/27, the different NHS pension tiers, and whether it’s truly worth it for your financial future.

What Is the NHS Pension Scheme?

The NHS Pension Scheme is a defined benefit (DB) occupational pension run by NHS England. Unlike a workplace defined contribution pension where your retirement pot depends on investment performance, a defined benefit scheme guarantees you a specific income in retirement based on your salary and length of service. This makes it exceptionally secure and valuable compared to most private sector alternatives.

Almost all NHS employees are eligible to join, including doctors, nurses, allied health professionals, and administrative staff. Membership is automatic for most employees, though you can opt out if you choose.

The 2015 CARE Scheme vs the Old Final Salary Scheme

Understanding which NHS pension scheme you are in is important, because the NHS has had more than one arrangement over the years.

The Old Final Salary Schemes (1995 and 2008 Sections)

Prior to 2015, NHS staff were enrolled in either the 1995 Section or the 2008 Section of the NHS Pension Scheme. Both were classic final salary arrangements:

  • Your pension was calculated based on your salary at retirement (or when you left the scheme).
  • The 1995 Section offered a normal pension age of 60, while the 2008 Section raised this to 65.
  • These sections are now closed to new entrants, but benefits already accrued in them are protected.

The 2015 CARE Scheme

Since 1 April 2015, all new NHS pension members — and most existing members following a legal ruling known as the McCloud Judgment remedy — now build up benefits in the 2015 Career Average Revalued Earnings (CARE) scheme. Here is how it works:

  • Each year, you earn a pension of 1/54th of your pensionable pay for that year.
  • Those annual “slices” of pension are added together and revalued each year in line with inflation (CPI plus 1.5% while you are an active member).
  • Your normal pension age is linked to your State Pension age (currently 67 for most people).
  • Because it is based on career average rather than final salary, it is fairer to staff who do not see large salary increases late in their career.

The McCloud Judgment remedy means that eligible members who were in the 1995 or 2008 Sections before 2015 will have their benefits compared under both the old and new rules at retirement, and they will receive whichever is the most beneficial — an important protection for longer-serving staff.

NHS Pension Contribution Rates 2026/27

As an NHS employee, you pay a percentage of your pensionable pay into the scheme each month. The rate you pay depends on which earnings tier your salary falls into. The NHS pension tiers for 2026/27 (England & Wales, effective 1 April 2026) are set out in the table below.

Pensionable Pay (per year) Employee Contribution Rate
Up to £13,259 5.2%
£13,260 to £28,854 6.5%
£28,855 to £35,155 8.3%
£35,156 to £52,778 9.8%
£52,779 to £67,668 10.7%
£67,669 and above 12.5%

These rates apply to your whole pensionable pay, not just the portion above each threshold — so your rate is determined by the band your total salary falls into. For example, a nurse earning £35,000 would pay 8.3% of their full £35,000 pensionable pay into the scheme.

These figures are for the England & Wales NHS Pension Scheme. Scotland (SPPA) and Northern Ireland (HSC) run separate schemes with different tier structures and are not covered by the table above — check with SPPA or HSC Pension Service directly if you work in Scotland or Northern Ireland.

What Does the Employer Contribute?

Here is where the NHS Pension Scheme becomes especially powerful. On top of your own contributions, your NHS employer contributes an additional 23.7% of your pensionable pay into the scheme on your behalf. This is an extraordinary level of employer support — far beyond what most private sector employers offer — and it dramatically increases the overall value of the pension you are building.

How Pension Tax Relief Works

One of the biggest advantages of contributing to the NHS Pension Scheme is pension tax relief. Your contributions are made from your pre-tax pay, which means:

  • If you are a basic rate taxpayer (20%), every £80 you contribute effectively costs you only £80 out of take-home pay, but you receive £100 worth of pension contribution because of the tax saved.
  • If you are a higher rate taxpayer (40%), the relief is even greater — a £100 contribution effectively costs you just £60 in reduced take-home pay.
  • For additional rate taxpayers (45%), the cost is just £55 for every £100 contributed.

The NHS Pension Scheme uses a salary sacrifice or notional relief arrangement depending on your employer, meaning in many cases the tax and National Insurance savings are applied automatically before you even see your payslip. This makes your pension contributions significantly more efficient than saving the same amount into a regular savings account.

Is the NHS Pension Worth It?

The short answer is: almost certainly yes. Here are the key reasons why most financial experts consider the NHS Pension Scheme outstanding value:

  • Guaranteed income in retirement: Unlike stocks and shares ISAs or defined contribution pensions, your NHS pension is not subject to market volatility. You know what you will get.
  • Massive employer contributions: The 23.7% employer top-up means the NHS is putting in more than most employees do themselves. This is free money you would lose by opting out.
  • Inflation protection: Your pension in payment rises with inflation (CPI), protecting your purchasing power throughout retirement.
  • Death and ill-health benefits: The scheme provides a lump sum on death in service and an ill-health retirement pension if you are unable to continue working due to a medical condition.
  • Dependants’ pensions: Your spouse, civil partner, or eligible dependants may receive a pension after your death.
  • Tax relief boosts your savings automatically: As explained above, every pound you contribute is enhanced by tax relief, making it highly efficient.

The main reason some NHS staff consider opting out is short-term cash flow — the contributions can feel substantial, particularly at the higher tiers. However, when you factor in the employer match and tax relief, opting out almost always leaves you worse off in the long run.

Key Takeaways

  • NHS pension contribution rates for 2026/27 range from 5.2% to 12.5% depending on your pay tier.
  • Your employer adds a further 23.7% on top — making total contributions exceptionally generous.
  • The 2015 CARE scheme is now the main scheme for active members, building pension at 1/54th of pay per year.
  • Pension tax relief means your contributions cost less than they appear on paper.
  • For the vast majority of NHS staff, staying in the pension scheme is the right financial decision.
